# Lab 12

In the Opening of the reading, we described a chimpanzee at a zoo who gathered stones in the morning and threw them at visitors hours later. In the Close, we said what behavior like that might require, and that for the chimpanzee no test that tells the possible procedures apart was ever run. This lab is about tests of that kind: what they can tell apart, and what they cannot.

In this lab you take three positions. In Part 1 you are the subject: you choose among gambles and delays, and you wait at two dispensers. In Part 2 you are first the subject and then the experimenter: you solve the Tower of Hanoi, run five procedures on the puzzle you just solved, and pay a point for every look ahead through trees of hidden numbers. In Part 3 you control a **hominin**, a member of the human line after it split from the line leading to chimpanzees and bonobos (§12.1.3), through two days on a landscape, and then you test five hidden hominins to find out what each one does. In Part 4 you grow corn in a model and compute heritability.

Two rules run through every part. The first is the rule of the lab, which you have followed since Lab 6: nothing an experiment would tell you is shown until you commit your answers. In Part 1 you do not learn what the choices measure until the last choice is made. In Part 3 you do not learn any hominin’s procedure until you have committed a diagnosis of all five. The second is a distinction we have kept since Lab 7. You are the only one in this lab who sees, remembers, and chooses. Every procedure on the puzzle, every looker, every hidden hominin, and the elder is a program with a procedure, and a line on each panel says so. Four of the hominins learn, and they learn by rules too.

Plan on about 90 minutes. The lab is built to be left and returned to, as explained under *How the lab is laid out*, below.

## Part 3: What They Carried (≈28 minutes)

### Goal

In §12.1.3 and §12.1.4 we described what *Homo erectus* carried, made, and used, and what the stones can and cannot show about how such behavior was organized. In §12.1.2 and §12.2.1 we described animals that act for a future need, and three procedures that could produce such behavior: an animal adapted to take each action in its own situation, with nothing linking the two; a value learned from one’s own past rewards; and a model of how the world works that is searched for a plan. Morgan’s Canon, which we introduced in Module 2, is the principle that a behavior should not be explained by a higher mental process if a simpler one accounts for it. For the zoo chimpanzee, no test that tells the procedures apart was run.

In Part 3, the chain of tests is your problem. First you spend two days on the range yourself, so that you learn its places, its items, and the timing of a morning. ## Part 4: Two Trays (≈15 minutes)

### Goal

In §12.3.5 we defined heritability as a statistic about a population: the proportion of the variation in a trait, in a particular population and set of environments, that is associated with genetic variation among its members. In §12.3.6 we described Lewontin’s two experiments with corn, which show why a high heritability within a group says nothing about what causes a difference between groups. In Part 4 you grow the corn yourself, in a model, run a twin study in a model population, and watch heritability change with the soil.
