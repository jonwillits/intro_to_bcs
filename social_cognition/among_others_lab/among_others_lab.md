# Lab 11

In the opening of the reading, and again in §11.1.10, we described a morning in a baboon group. Almost everything one baboon does has an effect that depends on what the other baboons do next, and what each of them does next depends on what it has seen, what it is trying to get, and what has happened between the two of them before. None of those things can be observed directly. What can be observed is behavior: where another animal goes, where it looks, and what it did on earlier occasions.

In this lab you take two positions. In Parts 1, 3, and 5 you are the subject. You will play a repeated game against six hidden partners and try to work out what each one does; you will watch three short films of moving shapes and judge where a dot in a maze is heading; and you will do two rounds of a task with the members of an invented animal. In Parts 2 and 4 you are the experimenter. You will run tournaments and populations of rules for the repeated game, and you will test five hidden animals in the food-competition arena of §11.1.9 and §11.2.3.

Two things run through every part. The first is the rule of the lab, which you have followed since Lab 6: nothing a test would tell you is shown until you commit your answers. In Part 1 you do not learn a partner's rule until you have committed a diagnosis of all six. In Part 3 you do not learn what the films or the judgments were for until the last answer is in. In Part 5 nothing is scored on the screen until both rounds are done and three predictions are committed. The second is a distinction we have kept since Lab 7. You are the only one in this lab who sees, remembers, and chooses. Every partner, every entry in a tournament, every cell of the grid, and every animal in the arena is a program with rules, and a line on each panel says so; the one of them that learns, the learner of Part 4, learns by a rule too.

Plan on about 95 minutes. The lab is built to be left and returned to, and the next section says how.

### What Part 1 tested

Each of your six partners followed one of six rules, and your seed decided which letter had which rule. In the reading, a strategy is a specification of what a player does in each situation the game can present (§11.1.7). Each of these rules is a strategy of the simplest kind: a first move, and a move for each of the four ways the previous round can have gone.

- **Always cooperate** cooperates on every round.
- **Always defect** defects on every round.
- **Tit-for-tat** cooperates on round 1, then plays your move from the previous round.
- **Defect forever after one defection** cooperates until the first round on which you defect, then defects on every later round. The literature calls this rule *grudger* or *grim trigger*; the screen names it by what it does.
- **Win-stay, lose-shift** cooperates on round 1; after a round that gave it 3 or 5 points, it repeats its own last move; after a round that gave it 0 or 1 points, it switches.
- **Suspicious tit-for-tat** defects on round 1, then plays your move from the previous round.

The results screen has four numbered sections. Section 1 shows each partner's rule beside your diagnosis, and counts your diagnoses right of 6. Section 2 is a table with one row per partner: the rounds played, your points, the partner's points, the number and share of your predictions that were right, the best possible total against that rule over that many rounds, and your share of the best. The best possible total is computed with the match's length, which you were not told, and against a partner that answers a defection it ends by defecting on the last round, so a gap of up to 2 points is expected against three of the rules. Section 3 is a scatter of the six partners, your share of predictions correct against your share of the best total, with a panel on the study of language models in repeated games that we described in §11.2.8. Section 4 is the record of every match, with the rounds on which the six rules could be told apart marked with an asterisk: a round is marked when, given the rounds before it, the six rules would not all have made the same move.

## Part 2: The Tournament (≈30 minutes)

### Goal

In §11.2.1 we described a tournament in which computer programs played the repeated prisoner's dilemma against one another, and the simplest program, tit-for-tat, won. In an evolutionary simulation, in which more successful programs were copied and less successful ones eliminated, tit-for-tat replaced all the others. Part 2 lets you run tournaments and simulations of that kind yourself, with the six rules of Part 1, a rule you build, and a new strategy that predicts its partners. Every number on the screen comes from the rules' moves and the payoff matrix, and nothing else.

Part 2 is an exploration. Each of its seven steps opens on a screen that explains what the step runs and asks you to predict the result before anything runs; press **“Commit my prediction”** on the left, and then the step's run button. You can leave a step and come back to it, and **“Redo Step n”** runs it again. The panel on the right shows the six rules of Part 1 as a table of first moves and the four responses, and your own rule beside them once you have built it.

Most of what you will see in this part is not reported in the reading. Where a result is new, the question below says what it is and where in the reading the idea behind it comes from. Several of the results are about **Predictor**, a strategy built for this lab, and about a cost charged for memory in Step 6; both are results of this simulation, and the questions say so.

## Part 4: What the Dominant Saw (≈15 minutes)

### Goal

In §11.1.9 we described experiments in which a subordinate and a dominant chimpanzee compete for pieces of food: the subordinate goes for food that a barrier hides from the dominant's view, and leaves alone food that the dominant has a clear view of. In §11.2.3 we set out the chain of tests behind that result, each of which ruled out a simpler explanation of the same choice, as **Morgan's Canon** asks: a behavior should not be explained by a higher mental process if a simpler one accounts for it. In §11.2.7 we set out five accounts of how one animal predicts another, and a sixth possibility: that a learner trained to predict behavior from enough examples might come to generalize to new situations, as a theory does.

Part 4 makes the chain of tests your problem. Five hidden animals, A to E, each follow a program with a rule of its own; which letter has which rule is your seed's. On the standard test, all five take the same piece of food. Your job is to choose the tests that tell them apart, diagnose each one, and then try a layout none of them has seen. These are programs with rules, and a line on the panel says so: the same choices do not show the same rule.

Before you begin, reread §11.1.9, §11.2.3, and §11.2.7 of the reading, and Morgan's Canon in Module 2.

### What Part 5 tested

The two rounds gave you the same task under two goals. In Round 1 your goal was to remember individual members.
