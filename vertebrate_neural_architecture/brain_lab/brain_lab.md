# Lab 6

Every lab so far has handed you an animal. Lab 1's vehicle, Lab 2's population, Lab 3's cell inside the vehicle, Lab 4's worm, Lab 5's worm with weights that move: each was one machine, and the question was always what it does and why. This week there is no animal to drive. Instead there are brains — real ones to identify, a stylized one to take apart, and a hidden map to find out by experiment — and the job is to discover that knowing where something happens in a brain is not the same as explaining it.

The reading's two big claims are both about anatomy, and both are claims you have to see to believe. The first is that the same parts turn up in every vertebrate brain: a lamprey's brain and a human's are built from the same pieces, in the same order, with one exception (§6.1.8, §6.3.5). What changed in half a billion years is mostly proportion and number. The second is that a part's address tells us much less than it seems to. A structure that is active during fear is active during many other things, and a structure needed for one job turns out to be needed for several (§6.2.1, §6.3.13). The reading calls the mistake reverse inference, and this lab is built to let you make it and then catch yourself making it.

Part 1 is comparative anatomy: a guessing game on photographs of thirteen brains, a tour of the parts on a model, and a tree to place the parts on. Part 2 is an experiment bench. You record from a generic mammal's brain during six tasks and write down what each structure is for, and then you take the animal apart with the other three tools and watch the table you wrote break. The closer asks you to design one experiment of your own.

## Part 2: An Address Is Not an Account (≈25 minutes)

### Goal

The reading's §6.2.1 says that recordings find the cerebellum and the basal ganglia active during movement, and also during perception, planning and thought, and that a region active during many different things licenses very little on its own. You are about to find that out from inside. First you will build a table of what each structure is for, using nothing but recordings, and it will look confident. Then you will break it, one entry at a time, with the other three tools.

**Read the bench before you use it.** Open the **Bench** tab. The animal is one generic mammal, not a named species. A gray cell is not an inactive structure. It is an honest gap. You will meet many of them, and you should notice where they are.
