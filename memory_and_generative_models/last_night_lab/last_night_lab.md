# Lab 10

In §10.1.1 of the reading, we described an early mammal back in its nest before dawn, with two kinds of fact about the night behind it. Some facts are true night after night: beetles are usually found under loose bark. Others are true for one night only: the beetles under this log were eaten last night. An animal that knows only what is usually true does not know what happened at that log last night, and on a particular night the usual place may not be the best place to look.

In this lab you take three positions. In Parts 1, 2, and 3 you are the subject of memory experiments: you study lists of words and type back what you remember, you study two sets of words in two ways, and you look at a room and then answer questions about what was in it. In Part 4 you are the small mammal of Lab 9, foraging at ten logs over six nights, where what you remember about last night decides where to dig tonight. In Part 5 you are the experimenter, and four programs forage on your own six nights: a tendency, a fast store of each night, and a slow learner, in the combinations the reading describes in §10.2.10.

Two things run through every part. The first is the rule of the lab, which you met in Labs 6 to 9: nothing a memory test would tell you is shown until you commit your answers. In Part 1 you do not learn what was on a list, or what the lists were for, until the last test is done. In Part 3 you do not see the room again until every question is answered. In Part 4 nothing about how a night went is shown until the week is over. The second is a distinction we have kept since Lab 7. You and the animal you drive are the only ones in this lab that may learn, choose, and remember. The foragers of Part 5 are programs given your nights, and a line on their panel reads the same.

Rats stand in for the early mammals of the reading, as they did in Lab 9. Most of what is known about memory for one night in a small mammal comes from rats in a radial maze (§10.1.1).

Plan on about 90 minutes. The lab is built to be left and returned to, and the next section says how.

### What Part 1 tested

Now that the results are up, here is what the part was built to measure. Four of the seven lists were lists of unrelated words. Three were made entirely of words associated with one word that was never shown, the critical word: a list of words like *bed, rest, awake, tired, dream*, all associated with *sleep*, which is not on the list (§10.2.12). The last test was a recognition test: 31 words, some from the lists, some new, and the three critical words among them. After each **“Old”** you said whether you **“Remember”** the word or **“Know”** it, in the sense the researchers who devised the test gave their subjects, whose sentences are printed on the results screen.

The results screen has six numbered sections. Section 1 shows each list with what you typed beside it, each typed word marked as a studied word, a word from an earlier list, a critical word, or other. Section 2 draws your serial-position curves: for the lists typed at once and for the lists typed after counting, the proportion of lists on which the word at each position was typed, with three bins behind each curve for the first three positions, the middle, and the last three. Section 3 counts the words you typed that were on an earlier list. Section 4 names the three critical words, says whether you typed each after its list, and gives the recognition test's proportions by kind of word, split by remember and know, beside the published proportions. Section 5 reports how often the first word you typed came from the last three positions. Section 6 records the presentation's timing.

The reading does not report serial-position curves, so here is what to look for. The rise at the first positions is called **primacy**, and the rise at the last positions **recency**. A word at the start of a list has the list to itself for a moment and can be repeated to oneself, which §10.2.3 calls rehearsal, before the next words crowd it. A word at the end has just been seen, and the three-store model of §10.2.2 says it is still in the short-term store, where it is lost within about 30 seconds unless repeated. Counting backward for 30 seconds is designed to take the short-term store away from you.

### What Part 2 tested

The two sets were of the same kind: 24 words from two top categories, each with three subcategories of four words. The difference was in how they were laid out. The first set was scrambled: the 24 words in a grid, in an order your seed set, with no labels. The second was organized: the same kind of set laid out as a tree, with its two top-category labels and six subcategory labels on the branches.

The results screen scores two things. The first is how many words you recalled from each set. The second is how much your own order of recall was clustered by subcategory, whatever the set looked like. The score is the adjusted ratio of clustering, ARC. It counts how often one word you typed was followed by another from the same subcategory, against what a random order would give: 1 is perfect clustering, every neighbor from the same subcategory, and 0 is the level chance would give. The screen works the score term by term for each set, with your own numbers in it, and a dash means the score is undefined for that set. Your typed words are shown colored by subcategory, with the set's words you did not type dimmed after them.

## Part 4: Nights at the Logs (≈22 minutes)

### Goal

In §10.1.1 we described the two kinds of fact early mammals have to work with, and in §10.1.3 we said what any system that learns about particular occasions must be able to do: recall a whole occasion from part of it, and distinguish two similar occasions, such as two nights at the same log. In §10.1.4 we described animals whose behavior depended on one occasion after it was over, and in §10.2.5 we said that researchers credit such animals with episodic-like memory on behavior alone, since re-living the occasion is probably undetectable from what an animal does.

Part 4 puts those problems to you, as the animal. You are the small mammal of Lab 9, back in its range at night. Out from your nest there are ten logs: seven with loose bark and three that are smooth. Each log looks different from every other, in its length, its thickness, its branch stubs, and its moss. Two rules hold through the whole part, and a fixed line beside the view repeats them. **The usual fact**: logs with loose bark have beetles under them unless the beetles have recently been eaten, and smooth logs never have beetles. **The refill rule**: after you empty a log, it is still empty the next night, and the night after that it has beetles again.

The six nights differ in their moon, their weather, what lies on the ground, and what you hear. Some nights look just like the night before in every one of those; some differ from the night before in every one. From night 3 on, foraging well depends on telling last night from the night before it: a log you emptied last night is empty, and a log you emptied two nights ago is full again. Nothing about how a night went is shown until the week is over.

## Part 5: Learners (≈20 minutes)
